Your visa status as a business owner in Japan is directly tied to your company's financial standing. If your registered capital drops below the threshold, or never met it in the first place, the 2026 changes have made your renewal position significantly more fragile than it was before. ---

What exactly changed in 2026 and who does it hit?

The minimum paid-in capital requirement for the Business Manager visa has been formally tightened. Under the updated enforcement standard effective from April 1, 2026, applicants must demonstrate registered capital of at least 5,000,000 yen at the time of both initial application and each renewal. This was the de facto standard before, but enforcement was inconsistent, and some officers accepted lower figures combined with compensating factors like employee headcount or revenue. That flexibility has narrowed. The new rules affect: - New applicants applying on or after April 1, 2026 - Renewal applicants whose current visa period expires after that date, regardless of when they first obtained the visa - Sole-director companies where the foreign national is the only officer and employee Who is grandfathered? Applicants mid-renewal cycle with an approval letter issued before April 1, 2026, and whose next renewal falls within a transitional review window, may still be evaluated under the prior standard. Confirm this in writing with your immigration attorney, not by assumption. ---

Why did the Immigration Bureau actually tighten this?

The stated rationale from the Ministry of Justice centers on eliminating shell company applications. Over the preceding years, a pattern emerged of foreigners establishing KK or GK entities with minimal capital, generating little or no revenue in Japan, and using the visa primarily as a residency vehicle rather than a genuine business operation. The 5,000,000 yen threshold now functions as a baseline credibility filter. Immigration officers are specifically trained to look at whether capital is paid-in capital actually sitting in the company account, not a personal account, not a loan dressed up as equity, not a parent company guarantee. "Proof of adequate capitalization" now legally means: a touzai transcript (登記事項証明書) showing the capital figure on record, cross-referenced against a company bank balance certificate from a Japanese bank issued within 3 months of your application date, and a shareholders' register confirming equity ownership structure. All three together. Missing one document is enough for a request for further evidence or outright denial. ---

Step-by-step: how to check if you are actually compliant

> [Step-by-Step Checklist] > > Step 1. Pull your current touzai transcript from the Legal Affairs Bureau (Homukyoku). Cost: 600 yen per sheet online via J-LIS system, or 480 yen at the counter. Confirm your registered capital figure. > > Step 2. If the figure is below 5,000,000 yen, calculate the shortfall. Example: registered at 3,000,000 yen, shortfall is 2,000,000 yen. > > Step 3. Contact your tax accountant or judicial scrivener (shiho shoshi) to initiate a capital increase procedure. Timeline: typically 2 to 4 weeks if documentation is clean. > > Step 4. Deposit the increase amount into the company account. Obtain a bank balance certificate (zandaka shomeisho) from your Japanese bank the same day or the next business day. > > Step 5. File the capital increase amendment at the Legal Affairs Bureau. Registration amendment fee: 30,000 yen. > > Step 6. Obtain a fresh touzai transcript after registration is confirmed. This typically takes 1 to 2 weeks to reflect. > > Step 7. Compile your shareholders' register showing the updated equity structure. > > Step 8. Submit all three documents together with your visa renewal application, within your renewal window. Do not submit your renewal application before the touzai transcript reflects the updated capital. If the document still shows the old figure, the officer reads what is in front of them, not your intent. ---

Raising your paid-in capital from below threshold to 5,000,000 yen is not just a paperwork exercise. Here is what the money looks like: - Legal Affairs Bureau registration amendment fee: 30,000 yen (fixed) - Judicial scrivener (shiho shoshi) service fee: 30,000 to 80,000 yen depending on complexity - Certified translation of shareholders' register (if originally in a foreign language): 5,000 to 15,000 yen per page - Bank balance certificate: most Japanese banks charge 0 to 1,100 yen per issuance - Fresh touzai transcript: 600 yen per document Total administrative and professional cost, excluding the actual capital you need to inject: roughly 70,000 to 130,000 yen in a straightforward case. The capital injection itself must be real money held in the company account. You cannot borrow it from a personal account on the day of the certificate and withdraw it the next day. Officers are experienced with this pattern. Banks sometimes flag unusual same-day transfers, and if your transaction history later draws scrutiny, it can create a finding of misrepresentation that is far more damaging than the original undercapitalization. ---

Who is most likely to get denied at renewal?

> [Warning Box] > > Highest-risk profiles for 2026 renewal denial: > - Sole-director companies with zero employees and registered capital below 5,000,000 yen > - Businesses generating revenue primarily from foreign clients with no Japan-registered contracts > - Companies where the foreign national director is physically outside Japan more than 180 days per year > - Operators who registered a company years ago and have not updated their touzai transcript since registration A concrete example: A Brazilian web developer operating a sole-director GK company registered in 2021 with 1,000,000 yen capital, serving clients in Brazil and the EU, with no Japan-based staff. She has renewed twice without issue. Under the 2026 enforcement standard, her next renewal application will be evaluated against the 5,000,000 yen threshold with no compensating headcount argument available. Based on common experience reported by foreign residents in similar situations, this profile now draws heightened scrutiny and documentation requests before a decision is issued. What if your company has revenue but not capital? Revenue does not substitute for paid-in capital. A company turning 20,000,000 yen in annual sales but registered with 500,000 yen capital is still non-compliant under the 2026 standard. You need both: adequate capitalization on the touzai transcript and operational activity. What if you incorporated offshore and operate in Japan? If you are running a foreign company with a Japan branch, different rules apply. A branch office does not have paid-in capital in the Japanese legal sense. The officer will assess the financial credibility of the parent company, typically requiring audited financials and a parent-company guarantee. This is a more complex application path and should not be attempted without legal counsel. My company was incorporated before 2026 with 2,000,000 yen capital. Am I automatically out of compliance? Not automatically, but you are at risk at your next renewal. The 2026 standard applies at the point of renewal evaluation, not at the point of original incorporation. You need to increase registered capital to 5,000,000 yen before submitting your next renewal application, with documents to prove it. Can I use a shareholder loan to meet the capital figure temporarily? No. Paid-in capital and shareholder loans are legally distinct. A loan sitting in the company account does not count as registered capital. The touzai transcript will show the actual registered capital figure, and no amount of bank balance will compensate for a discrepancy there. If my renewal is denied, what happens to my legal status immediately? A denial does not make you immediately illegal if you applied within your current valid status period. You have a short window to appeal or depart voluntarily. However, a denial on record complicates every future immigration application, including PR.
