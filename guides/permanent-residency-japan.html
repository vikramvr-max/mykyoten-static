Japan PR Rules Changing Oct 2026: Income, Pension, Language | MyKyoten

Japan's PR fee jumps from 10,000 to 200,000 yen on October 1, 2026, alongside new income, pension, and language thresholds. Here is what changes, who it…

On August 4, 2026, Japan's Immigration Services Agency published draft guidelines that tighten permanent residency requirements across five areas: income, pension, Japanese language ability, visa status, and school attendance for applicants' children. The stated goal, according to the agency, is to build what officials describe as an orderly society where Japanese and foreign residents coexist. The practical effect is a materially harder application, and a much more expensive one.

The headline number is the fee. Permanent residency applications currently cost 10,000 yen. From October 1, 2026, that rises to 200,000 yen, a twentyfold increase. Some statutory reduction cases may qualify for a lower 20,000 yen fee, but for most applicants the jump is the full amount. If you are anywhere close to eligible, the fee alone is a reason to understand your timing before the deadline, not after.

This guide breaks down what is actually confirmed, what is still a draft proposal that could change, and what that means for your specific pathway to PR.

As of this writing, in early September 2026, this is a draft guideline revision, not a finalized law. The Immigration Services Agency opened the proposal for public comment on August 4, 2026, and that comment period ran for about a month, closing in early September. The agency has said it expects to finalize and formally publish the guidelines around October 1, 2026, the same date the changes are meant to take effect.

That means two things worth sitting with. First, the specific figures, particularly the exact income benchmark, have not been officially published. Some coverage has cited a reference figure near 5.75 million yen tied to average Japanese household income, but the agency itself has not confirmed a specific yen amount as of this writing. Treat that number as directional, not final, until the agency publishes it. Second, because this is close to finalization rather than an early trial balloon, the direction is unlikely to reverse even if individual figures shift slightly. Planning around the general shape of these changes, rather than waiting for every number to be locked in, is the more realistic approach.

One detail matters more than it might seem: the agency has indicated the income and pension standards may apply to applications filed as early as April 2026 that are still pending when the new framework takes effect. If you filed this year and your application is still under review, this is not purely a future problem.

Applications accepted by September 30, 2026 remain subject to the 10,000 yen fee even if the actual permission is granted later. Applications accepted on or after October 1, 2026 are subject to the new 200,000 yen fee. The word accepted matters here, it refers to when your application is received by immigration, not when you started preparing it or when a decision comes back.

If your documentation is close to ready and you meet the current requirements, filing before October 1 locks in both the old fee and, in practice, likely the current eligibility standard rather than the tightened one, since your application would already be in process before the new framework applies to new filings. If you are not close to ready, rushing an incomplete or weak application before the deadline is generally worse than waiting and applying properly under the new rules, since a rejected application costs you the fee either way and resets nothing in your favor. Filing early only helps if the application is actually strong.

Under the draft guidelines, applicants would need to continuously demonstrate income exceeding the average annual household income of Japanese households of a comparable size, rather than meeting a flat minimum figure. This is a meaningful shift from how income has practically been assessed. Under the current, unofficial standard used by many immigration offices, roughly 3 million yen a year for a single applicant with no dependents has been the widely cited floor, rising with household size. The new standard ties the bar to the average Japanese household instead, which for most applicants means a materially higher number.

The Immigration Services Agency has not published the specific statistical benchmark it will use. Some reporting has referenced a figure around 5.75 million yen, but that number should be treated as a media estimate, not an official published threshold, until the agency confirms it.

What this means practically: if your income currently sits comfortably above the old 3 to 4.5 million yen practical range but below a household-average benchmark in the mid 5 million yen area, your application that would have looked strong under the old standard could look borderline under the new one. This is worth checking now, not after you have already gathered your documents.

A new requirement under the draft guidelines assesses whether an applicant's expected future pension benefit is equivalent to that of someone with 30 years of Employees' Pension Insurance (kosei nenkin) enrollment, roughly in the range of 1.3 to 1.6 million yen a year in projected benefit.

This is the change most likely to catch long-term residents off guard, specifically. If you spent your first several years in Japan on national pension (kokumin nenkin) rather than employees' pension, either because you were a student, self-employed, or working part-time, your projected benefit under that history is structurally lower than someone who was on employees' pension the whole time, even if you never missed a single payment. This is not about compliance gaps, it is about which pension system you were enrolled in and for how long.

Understanding the difference between national and employees' pension matters more under this framework than it did before. If you have any uncovered months, close them now. You can retroactively pay up to 2 years of missed national pension contributions, and doing so before you apply is one of the few concrete actions available to you under this new standard.

The draft guidelines introduce Japanese language proficiency at JLPT N2 or CEFR B1 level as part of a broader integration framework that also considers school attendance for applicants' children of compulsory education age. This is a genuinely new category of requirement for standard PR applications, previously, language ability only mattered indirectly, through bonus points on the Highly Skilled Professional route.

If you are not already near N2 level, this is the requirement with the longest lead time to satisfy, language study takes months to years, not weeks. If PR is part of your plan and your Japanese is currently below N2, starting now rather than after the guidelines finalize is the only way to have this ready when you actually apply. This applies regardless of which pathway you are on, the standard 10-year route, the HSP fast track, or the spouse route.

Currently, if you are married to a Japanese national, you can apply for PR after just 1 year of residence on a spouse visa, provided you have been married for at least 3 years total, including time spent married while living outside Japan.

Under the draft guidelines, this would change to 5 years of marriage and 3 years of residence in Japan, up from the current 3 years of marriage and 1 year of residence. For applicants relying on this pathway, that is a meaningful delay, not a minor adjustment. If you are currently on the spouse route and close to your 1-year residence mark under the existing rule, filing before the new framework takes effect is worth prioritizing, since the alternative may mean waiting an additional two years under the revised requirement.

Beyond the spouse route above, there are two other main pathways, and the new rules apply to both, though the mechanics of each stay largely the same.

This is the default. You need 10 continuous years of residence (see reporting compliance requirements to ensure continuity) in Japan, with at least 5 of those years on a work visa or qualifying residence status, not student, not trainee. Student years do not count toward the 5-year work requirement even if you held a part-time job.

Continuous means continuous, and immigration is specific about what breaks it. A single trip abroad longer than 3 months breaks continuity outright and resets your clock to zero. Multiple shorter trips totaling more than 150 days in a year also draw scrutiny. Based on common experience, stays under 30 days are rarely questioned, while trips of 60 to 90 days need solid documentation of your reason for travel. Your 5 work years do not need to be consecutive, but they must fall within the 10-year continuous residence window.

If you score 70 points or more on Japan's HSP points system, you can apply for PR after 3 years of residence. Score 80 or more, and the wait drops to just 1 year.

Here is the catch: you do not need to currently hold an HSP visa to use this pathway. If you can demonstrate that you would have qualified for 70 or 80 points at the relevant time in the past, 3 years ago or 1 year ago, you can apply under this route even while holding a regular work visa. This is a detail that many applicants miss entirely.

Points come from five categories on the official METI point calculation sheet: academic background, professional career, annual salary, age, and bonus factors. A doctoral degree adds 30 points, a master's adds 20. Age under 30 adds 15 points, under 35 adds 10. Under the new language framework, Japanese ability now carries weight on two fronts at once, it already added bonus points toward your 70 or 80 point score, and it may become a standalone requirement regardless of your total score.

Your points must be verifiable at the time you submit the application, not just at the time your original HSP visa was granted. If your salary has dropped, or your employer lost a certification your bonus points depended on, immigration scores you on what they can confirm from your submitted documents on application day. A drop from 80 to 70 points simply moves you to the 3-year track, it is not a rejection, but it changes your timeline, and now potentially your fee bracket too.

Only if you are actually ready. Filing an application in September to catch the old fee and old standard makes sense if your documentation is genuinely complete and your income, pension, and residence history are already clean. It does not make sense if you would be filing a weak application just to beat the deadline.

A rejected application does not refund the fee, and it creates a paper trail immigration will reference on any future application. Filing under pressure, with gaps in your financial record you have not yet addressed, is very likely to produce a worse outcome than filing a clean application in November under the new rules. The one genuine exception is the spouse route timing described above, where the eligibility window itself, not just the fee, is closing.

If you are within a few months of being genuinely ready under the current standard, that is the scenario where moving quickly has real value. If you are not, spend the next few months closing pension gaps, confirming your income trajectory, and, if relevant, working toward N2, then apply once, properly, under whichever rules are in effect.

This was true before the rule changes and remains true now, arguably more so. The single most common reason for PR rejection has been financial record problems, not criminal issues, not insufficient residency. This includes proper job change reporting and maintaining clean records.

The type of income matters as much as the amount. A salaried employee at a stable Japanese company with full social insurance enrollment presents a very different risk profile than a freelancer reporting the same number. Salary and consistent bonuses count. Rental income counts if declared on your tax return with a paper trail. Spousal income can also count toward the household picture under the household-average framework, and is an underused option, even when your spouse is not the primary applicant.

Immigration will request your tax payment certificates going back multiple years. They want to see that you paid the correct amount, on time, for every year. Late payments show up on these certificates. Even payments made one month late can appear as delinquent for the period in which they were due. Understanding resident tax obligations is critical before applying.

Immigration now routinely requests your nenkin records. If you were on national pension at any point and missed payments, those gaps are visible. If you switched between national pension and employees' pension during job transitions and there is an uncovered month in between, that shows up too.

If you have gaps, you can retroactively pay up to 2 years of missed national pension contributions. Do this before you apply. Learn more about the pension withdrawal system if you're leaving Japan instead.

Request your own nenkin record from the pension office and your nozei shomeisho from the city office before you apply. Review them yourself. Fix any discrepancies before immigration sees them, and before you find out under the new pension-equivalence standard that years of national pension enrollment left you short.

A PR application is not just a stack of papers. It is a narrative. Every document either strengthens or weakens the story of a stable, contributing, long-term resident.

The guarantor needs to be a Japanese national or PR holder. A spouse works. An employer or senior colleague works. A friend with shaky finances does not help your case.

Your employer's letter should state your position, your tenure, your salary, and ideally a line about expected continued employment. A vague letter that omits salary or role details is not enough.

Inconsistencies between documents are what make applications look weak. If your tax certificate shows a different income than your employer letter, that raises questions. Immigration officers cross-reference. Assume they will find any discrepancy.

Rejections have followed clear patterns even before this year's changes. Tax delinquency is the top reason, including late payments, underpayments, and years where you failed to file a tax return. Job instability is the second most common issue, immigration wants to see that you can sustain yourself indefinitely. Visa status irregularities matter too, if you overstayed even briefly in the past, or worked outside the conditions of a previous visa, that history is in the system.

Case study: David, a Canadian software engineer, applied for PR after 10 years in Japan. Strong salary, stable job, Japanese spouse. His application was rejected. The reason: he had changed addresses three times in two years with late notifications, plus one uncovered pension month during a job transition. Always follow the 14-day address change reporting rule. After correcting the pension gap and maintaining a stable address for 14 months, he reapplied and was approved in 4 months.

Yes. PR can be revoked, and separately from the application changes above, the government has also issued new guidelines on revocation criteria under the 2024 immigration law amendments. Long absences of more than 1 year without a re-entry permit expire your PR automatically. With a re-entry permit, you can stay abroad for up to 5 years, but immigration can still question whether you have genuinely maintained Japan as your primary residence. Understand the visa revocation procedures to avoid risks.

A criminal conviction in Japan can lead to PR revocation and deportation, drug offenses are treated with extreme severity. On tax and pension delinquency after approval, the newer guidance does include some exemptions, nonpayment caused by illness, unemployment, disaster, or other genuinely unavoidable circumstances would not normally trigger revocation, and applicants who respond to payment demands and agree to installment or deferral arrangements are generally excluded as well. Ignoring the problem entirely is what creates real risk, not a documented hardship.

Before applying, verify each of these:

Tax: filed and paid for all years of Japanese residence, no late filings, no underpayments.

Pension: no uncovered months, no late payments, any gaps retroactively paid within the 2-year window, and a rough sense of whether your pension history clears the new 30-year-equivalent benchmark.

Language: current JLPT level identified, and a realistic plan in place if you are below N2 and the language requirement finalizes as proposed.

Income: consistent salary trajectory, and checked against both the old practical floor and the new household-average direction, even without an official figure yet.

Address: stable address history, all changes reported within 14 days.

Documentation: guarantor confirmed, employer letter contains role, tenure, salary, and contract status, all documents consistent with each other.

Timing: a clear decision on whether filing before September 30, 2026 makes sense for your situation, or whether you are better served waiting and filing a genuinely complete application after.
