So How Does the Municipality Actually Calculate What You Owe?

Resident tax in Japan is not calculated per employer. It is calculated on your total annual income from all sources combined.

Here is why that matters. Your municipality receives income data from your 確定申告 (final tax return) filed by March 15. It then adds up every yen you earned across every job, every freelance contract, every side gig. That combined figure is what they use to calculate your resident tax for the coming year. The standard rate is 10% of taxable income (6% prefectural, 4% municipal), plus a per-capita flat levy that typically ranges from 3,000 to 5,000 yen annually depending on your municipality.

The bracket effect is real. If Job A pays you 3,500,000 yen and Job B pays you 800,000 yen, your resident tax is calculated on 4,300,000 yen of combined income minus applicable deductions. Each employer, if taxed separately, would have withheld based on a lower income figure. The combined bill is larger than the sum of the individual deductions would have been. The municipality does not alert you to this in advance. The bill simply arrives.

That gap is the surprise lump sum that catches people off guard.

Why Can Only One Employer Collect It?

The system is called 特別徴収 (special collection). Your municipality issues a 特別徴収税額通知書 (resident tax withholding instruction notice) to one employer, instructing them to deduct your full annual resident tax in 12 equal monthly instalments from June through May of the following year.

Only one employer receives this notice. There is no mechanism under the current system to split the deduction across multiple payrolls automatically.

The municipality typically assigns 特別徴収 to your main employer, meaning the one listed on your 確定申告 as your primary source of salary income. That employer then deducts the full annual amount. Because that amount reflects your total income from all sources, the monthly deduction will appear disproportionately high relative to the salary that employer is actually paying you.

A Brazilian graphic designer on an Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services visa, earning 3,200,000 yen at a Tokyo design firm and an additional 600,000 yen from freelance contracts, would see their design firm deducting resident tax calculated on roughly 3,800,000 yen of income. The firm cannot see the income breakdown. They only see the deduction amount. Based on reports from foreign residents, this discrepancy frequently prompts HR departments to ask questions.

Warning Box If your visa does not permit side work and your main employer's resident tax deduction is noticeably higher than your salary with them would justify, your employer may flag the discrepancy with HR or payroll. This is one documented path by which undisclosed side income becomes visible to an employer who did not know about it. It does not trigger an automatic immigration report, but employer knowledge creates its own risks depending on your contract terms and visa category.

Can You Keep the Side Income Portion Private?

There is a legal mechanism. When filing your 確定申告, you can request that the portion of resident tax attributable to income other than your main employer's salary be collected via 普通徴収 (ordinary collection), meaning you pay it yourself by bank transfer or furikomi rather than having it deducted through payroll.

On the paper 確定申告 form (第二表), there is a section asking how you want resident tax collected. You check the box requesting 普通徴収 for income other than salary. On the e-Tax filing system, the equivalent option appears in the resident tax section of the form.

This request does not guarantee the split. Municipalities are not legally required to honour it for all income types. Based on common experience reported by foreign residents, major urban municipalities like those in Tokyo, Osaka, and Nagoya are more likely to accommodate the split for freelance or business income. Some smaller municipalities assign everything to 特別徴収 regardless. There is no official published list of which municipalities accept or reject this request.

If the split is approved, you receive two separate notices. One goes to your employer for the salary-based portion. One comes to your home address for the side income portion, and you pay it in four instalments (June, August, October, January).

Step-by-Step Checklist: After Filing Your 確定申告

  • File your 確定申告 by March 15, including all income sources
  • In the resident tax section, check the 普通徴収 box for non-salary income
  • Keep a copy of your filed return as evidence of the request
  • Wait for the resident tax notice to arrive at your registered address in late May or June
  • Open the notice immediately and verify whether the municipality split the collection method
  • Check the 特別徴収 amount assigned to your employer against what you expected
  • If the full amount was assigned to 特別徴収 against your request, contact your municipality tax office within two to three weeks of the notice date

What Happens Between March 15 and the June Notice?

The timeline moves quietly. After you file by March 15, your municipality processes the data and issues resident tax assessments for the following fiscal year. The notices go out in late May, with deductions starting from the June salary payment.

You will not receive a warning or a preliminary estimate. The first signal that something is different from what you expected is the notice itself, or the deduction appearing on your June payslip.

Concrete example: A Canadian English teacher on a Humanities visa files her 確定申告 in February, reporting 2,900,000 yen from her main school employer and 420,000 yen from private online tutoring. She requests 普通徴収 for the tutoring income. Her municipality calculates resident tax on the combined 3,320,000 yen, producing an annual resident tax bill of approximately 237,000 yen (simplified, before deductions). If the municipality honours her request, roughly 190,000 yen is collected through her employer at about 15,833 yen per month, and the remaining amount is billed to her directly. If the municipality rejects the split, the full 237,000 yen is deducted through her employer payroll, and HR notices that the monthly deduction of approximately 19,750 yen seems high for her declared salary.

What If the Municipality Assigns Everything to Your Employer Anyway?

You have limited but real options.

First, contact the municipal tax office (市区町村の税務課) directly, as soon as possible after receiving the notice. Bring your filed 確定申告 showing the 普通徴収 request. Some municipalities will correct the assignment before the June deductions begin. Once June starts, reversing the assignment mid-year is significantly harder.

Second, understand that "harder" does not mean impossible. Based on common experience, municipalities will sometimes convert the side income portion to 普通徴収 mid-year if you have a documented reason and the employer has not yet begun deductions. This requires an in-person visit and written request in most cases.

What if you simply did not file a 確定申告? If you had side income above 200,000 yen and did not file, the municipality may still receive income data from clients or platforms that filed their own 支払調書 (payment records). The resident tax assessment arrives based on that data. You cannot retroactively request 普通徴収 on income you did not declare yourself. The full amount gets assigned to 特別徴収 through your main employer, and you are also potentially liable for late filing penalties on the income tax side.

Reality Check The 200,000 yen threshold that exempts salaried workers from filing a 確定申告 for side income does not mean that income is invisible to your municipality. Municipalities calculate resident tax based on all income including amounts below 200,000 yen. Filing solely for resident tax purposes, using a juminzei申告 (住民税申告) at your municipal office, is an option some residents use. It does not substitute for national income tax filing when required, but it does give you the opportunity to make the 普通徴収 request.

What Does This Mean If You Are on a Visa With Side Work Restrictions?

This section is not theoretical. It is the part that catches people out.

Visa categories in Japan carry specific work authorization. An Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services visa authorizes work in activities matching your designated activities. A Highly Skilled Professional visa has its own scope conditions. A Student visa permits up to 28 hours of side work per week under a Resource Activity permission. Spouse of Japanese National visas generally permit broader employment but are not unlimited in all cases.

If your side income comes from work that your visa does not permit, the tax system does not care. The municipality calculates and bills you regardless. But the visibility created by a resident tax assessment is a real administrative paper trail.

The specific risk for foreigners: If your main employer receives a 特別徴収 notice that reflects substantially more income than your salary with them produces, and they escalate that internally, your undisclosed side work may become known. Separately, if your undisclosed work surfaces during a visa renewal application through inconsistencies between declared income and tax records, it creates problems that are difficult to resolve after the fact. Your next step: If you have already filed your 確定申告 with multiple income sources and you are unsure whether your 普通徴収 request was correctly entered, pull out your copy of the filed form and check the resident tax section before the June notices arrive. If you are approaching the filing period and have not yet sorted your income sources, do that reconciliation now, not after the municipal notices land. The window to influence how your resident tax is collected is the 確定申告 form itself. Once the assessment is issued, the room to change it is narrow.

If my side job paid less than 200,000 yen, do I still have a resident tax problem?

Yes. The 200,000 yen national income tax filing exemption does not apply to resident tax. Your municipality calculates resident tax on all income. If no 確定申告 was filed, file a 住民税申告 directly with your municipal office before the end of the fiscal year to ensure accurate calculation and to make the 普通徴収 request.

Can my employer legally ask me why my resident tax deduction is so high?

Yes, employers frequently ask. You are not legally required to disclose which other income sources exist, but HR departments do notice. How you respond depends on your employment contract terms and whether your side work is permitted under your visa and contract. There is no universal prohibition on the employer asking.

What happens if I just pay my resident tax late or ignore the self-payment notice?

Late payment of 普通徴収 instalments incurs a late penalty of roughly 2.4% to 8.9% annually depending on how overdue the payment is, under the Local Tax Act. Persistent non-payment can lead to asset seizure, and unpaid resident tax will appear in your tax compliance record, which affects visa renewal and PR applications.
