Dependent Deduction in Japan for Foreigners

Japanese tax law allows residents, including foreign nationals, to deduct a fixed amount from taxable income for each qualifying dependent living in Japan or abroad.

If you are sending money home to support your parents or family every month but have never claimed them as dependents on your Japanese tax return, you are paying thousands of yen more in income tax and resident tax than the law requires, because almost no one tells foreigners this deduction exists for overseas family.

So What Actually Is the Overseas Dependent Deduction?

Japanese tax law allows residents, including foreign nationals, to deduct a fixed amount from taxable income for each qualifying dependent. This applies whether that dependent lives in Japan or abroad. The legal framework is the same.

A dependent, under Japan's Income Tax Act, is a relative within the sixth degree of kinship (or within the third degree by marriage) who shares your livelihood financially. The key phrase is *shares your livelihood*. For overseas family members, this means you are the primary financial supporter.

**The deduction amounts break down by age:**

- General dependent (16 to 18, and 23 to 69): ¥380,000 per person - Specified dependent child (19 to 22): ¥630,000 per person - Elderly dependent (70 and over, living separately): ¥480,000 per person - Elderly dependent (70 and over, living with you or a direct relative): ¥580,000 per person

One distinction matters immediately. **Children under 16 are excluded from the income tax dependent deduction entirely.** However, they can still be claimed for resident tax (住民税) purposes in some municipalities. If you have young children overseas, ask your municipal office specifically about resident tax, since it is a separate calculation.

The dependent's own income must be under ¥480,000 per year (for the 2025 tax year onward, following wage base adjustments). If your parent back home earns above that threshold, they generally cannot be claimed.

The 2023 Rule Change That Every Foreign Resident Needs to Know

This is not optional background. This rule change directly affects whether your claim will be accepted.

Starting from the 2023 tax year (filed in early 2024), the Japanese government introduced stricter conditions for overseas dependents aged **30 to 69**. Before this amendment, foreigners could claim overseas family members in this age group with relatively minimal documentation. The rules tightened specifically because tax authorities identified a pattern of mass filings by foreign nationals that could not be independently verified.

To claim an overseas dependent aged 30 to 69, you must now prove at least one of the following:

1. The dependent is a full-time student enrolled in an overseas educational institution 2. The dependent has a recognized disability 3. You sent the dependent remittances of **¥380,000 or more** during the calendar year

In practice, condition three is the most commonly used. That means your bank transfer records are not just helpful. They are essential. If you cannot show documented remittances totalling at least ¥380,000 for the year, your claim for a parent in that age bracket will likely be denied.

The rule does not apply to dependents under 30 or 70 and over, where standard income and relationship proof remains sufficient.

> **Warning Box:** If you claimed overseas dependents aged 30 to 69 before 2023 and did not keep remittance records, and if you are now being asked to justify those past returns, the standard lookback period is five years. Claims from 2019 onward may be reviewed. Penalties for unjustified deductions include back taxes plus a surcharge of 10% to 15% of the underpaid amount, and in cases of deliberate misreporting, up to 35% additional penalty tax.

Exactly Which Documents You Need to Submit

Getting the documents right is where most foreign residents struggle. The tax office expects specific evidence in two categories: **proof of relationship** and **proof of financial support**.

- A birth certificate connecting you to the dependent (for parents, your own birth certificate showing their names) - A family register (戸籍謄本 equivalent from your home country) - Marriage certificate if the dependent is a spouse - Documents must be translated into Japanese. Self-translation is technically permitted by the National Tax Agency, but based on reports from foreign residents, some tax offices push back on self-translated documents. A professionally translated or notarized translation is considerably safer and costs roughly ¥5,000 to ¥15,000 per document depending on complexity and the translation agency.

**Proof of financial support:**

- International remittance receipts from your bank showing the recipient's name, amount, and date - Online banking PDF statements showing wire transfers - If you used a transfer service like Wise, a transaction history printout that clearly shows name, amount in yen, and destination is generally acceptable

**What if your home country does not issue documents that match Japanese expectations?**

This is real and common. Some countries issue documents in formats that Japanese tax offices are unfamiliar with, or documents that combine multiple family relationships in one registry that does not translate cleanly. In these cases:

- Obtain an apostille where your country is a Hague Convention signatory - Contact your country's embassy in Japan and request an official certification letter confirming the family relationship - Bring both the original document and translation. A cover letter from yourself explaining the document format, while not officially required, helps in practice

How Do You Actually File This: Through Your Employer or By Yourself?

Most salaried foreign employees in Japan go through **年末調整 (year-end tax adjustment)**, which your employer handles in November or December. Your HR department will distribute forms, including one that asks about dependents. This is where you declare overseas family members.

The problem: HR departments at smaller companies are often unequipped to handle foreign-language supporting documents. Based on common experience reported by foreign residents, some HR teams simply reject any document they cannot read in Japanese, without escalating it. If this happens, do not accept the rejection as final.

**Your options when an employer rejects your overseas dependent claim:**

1. Submit a certified Japanese translation of your documents and resubmit 2. File your own 確定申告 directly with the tax office after year-end, which allows you to claim deductions your employer missed 3. File a correction return (更正の請求) within five years if you realize after the fact that you missed the deduction

Filing 確定申告 independently gives you more control. The tax office window period runs from mid-February to mid-March each year for the previous calendar year. Online filing via e-Tax is available and does not require in-person document submission for most cases, though the tax office may request originals if your return is flagged.

**A concrete example:** A Vietnamese software engineer on an Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services visa earns ¥5,500,000 per year and supports his 58-year-old mother in Hanoi. She has no income. He sends her ¥50,000 per month via bank wire, totalling ¥600,000 for the year. His mother is in the 30 to 69 age bracket, so the 2023 rules apply. He has remittance receipts exceeding ¥380,000, a Vietnamese birth certificate listing his mother's name, and a Japanese translation he paid ¥8,000 to have certified. His eligible deduction is ¥380,000, which at his marginal income tax rate of 20% reduces his income tax by approximately ¥76,000. His resident tax drops separately by roughly ¥38,000. Combined annual savings: approximately ¥114,000.

What Happens If Your Claim Gets Audited or You Made a Mistake?

The National Tax Agency does audit dependent deductions, particularly overseas ones. If selected, you will receive a written inquiry (お尋ね) asking you to supply supporting documents within a set timeframe, usually 20 to 30 days.

If you cannot substantiate the claim, the deduction is disallowed. You will owe back taxes plus:

- A **10% under-reporting surcharge** on the additional tax owed - A **15% surcharge** if the underpayment exceeds ¥500,000 or half the declared tax - In cases of deliberate false filing: a **35% heavy penalty surcharge**

Daily delinquency interest (延滞税) also accrues at roughly 2.4% to 8.7% per year depending on the period.

**What if you forgot to claim overseas dependents in past years?**

You can file a 更正の請求 (request for correction) to recover overpaid tax. The lookback window is **five years** from the original filing deadline. If you have been sending money home and have remittance records going back several years but never claimed the deduction, you may be able to recover substantial amounts. The five-year window means 2020 tax year returns filed in 2021 are recoverable if you act before the 2026 deadline. This is worth calculating carefully.

**Edge case one:** Your dependent aged 35 is a university student overseas but has no official enrollment certificate in Japanese. You will need an enrollment letter from the university, translated, showing full-time student status. Without this, the student exemption to the 30 to 69 rule cannot be confirmed, and the remittance route becomes your fallback.

**Edge case two:** You sent money home but used cash courier transfers or informal hawala-style remittance networks with no paper trail. These transfers, even if real, cannot be verified by the tax office. Only traceable, documented bank or registered remittance service transfers are accepted.

**Can I claim my sibling overseas as a dependent?**

Yes, if the sibling is within the sixth degree of kinship (which siblings are) and meets the income and support conditions. You still need to prove the relationship and your financial support with the same documentation requirements. The 30 to 69 age rule applies equally.

**My employer's HR said overseas dependents are not allowed. Is that correct?**

No. Japanese tax law explicitly permits overseas dependents for all tax residents, including foreign nationals. HR departments sometimes misapply the rules. If your employer rejects a valid claim, file 確定申告 directly with your local tax office and claim the deduction yourself.

**Do I need to re-submit documents every year?**

Generally yes. The tax office expects fresh documentation each year confirming the dependent relationship and financial support. Some offices accept prior-year documents if the situation is unchanged, but submitting updated remittance records annually is standard practice and protects your claim.
