You paid tax on that income in another country. Then Japan taxed it again. That is not a misunderstanding. That is exactly how Japan's tax system works for residents. The foreign tax credit (外国税額控除) exists precisely to stop this from becoming a permanent double cost. Most foreigners either do not know it exists or file without claiming it. ---

Who Actually Gets Hit With Double Taxation in Japan?

Japan taxes residents on their **worldwide income**. The rule applies once you are classified as a resident for tax purposes, which generally means you have an address in Japan and have been here for more than five years in the past ten years. At that point, income you earned anywhere on earth is reportable on your Japanese return. If you have been in Japan for five years or less, you fall under the **non-permanent resident** category. Non-permanent residents are only taxed on Japan-source income and on foreign-source income that is remitted to Japan. Once you cross that five-year threshold, every yen of foreign income is in scope, regardless of whether you brought it into a Japanese bank account. This is where the collision happens. A US brokerage withholds 10% or 15% from your dividends before you ever see them. The UK takes income tax from your rental profit. Germany withholds tax on freelance payments before transferring the net. Then Japan says: report all of that. Pay Japanese income tax on all of it. If you do nothing, you lose. You have already paid a foreign government, and now you pay Japan on top. If you claim the foreign tax credit correctly, the Japanese tax you owe on that foreign income is reduced by the amount you already paid abroad, up to the limit the formula allows. Understanding your residency category matters for more than just this credit. If you are working toward [permanent residency in Japan](https://mykyoten.com/guides/permanent-residency-japan), your tax filing history is part of the evaluation. ---

How the Foreign Tax Credit Actually Reduces What You Owe

The credit does not give you back the foreign tax directly. It offsets your Japanese tax liability. The formula the National Tax Agency uses: **Creditable Limit = (Foreign-Source Income / Total Income) x Japanese Income Tax** Then there is a separate calculation for resident tax (住民税), which uses the same structure but applies to the resident tax component. Here is a concrete example. A Canadian data scientist on an Engineer/Specialist in Humanities visa earns 8,000,000 yen from her Japanese employer and receives 400,000 yen in Canadian dividend income. Canada withheld 60,000 yen equivalent in withholding tax. Her total Japanese income tax before credits is 1,200,000 yen. Creditable limit = (400,000 / 8,400,000) x 1,200,000 = **57,142 yen** She cannot claim the full 60,000 yen she paid Canada. Her creditable limit is 57,142 yen. That is the maximum offset she gets. The remaining 2,858 yen cannot be carried forward under most circumstances. This is the critical point most people miss: **the credit cannot exceed what Japan would have charged you on that same income**. If you paid more tax abroad than Japan would have charged, you absorb the difference. --- > **Warning Box:** If your foreign tax rate is significantly higher than Japan's rate on the same income, you will not recover the full foreign tax through this credit. The credit only offsets Japanese tax. It does not generate a refund beyond your Japanese liability. ---

Which Foreign Taxes Qualify and Which Do Not

**Taxes that qualify:** - National income taxes paid to a foreign government - Taxes confirmed as substantially equivalent to Japanese income tax under a tax treaty or NTA guidance - Withholding taxes on dividends, interest, and royalties paid to a foreign government at source **Taxes that do not qualify:** - Foreign social insurance contributions (pension, health insurance equivalents) - Value-added tax or consumption tax equivalents - Penalties and late fees imposed by a foreign tax authority - Taxes that are refundable under the foreign country's own system (if you are entitled to claim them back, Japan does not credit them) - Taxes paid to foreign local governments in some cases, depending on treaty provisions The documentation challenge is real. Some countries issue a clear tax payment certificate. Others do not. Based on common experience reported by foreign residents, the most reliable documents are: official withholding tax statements from brokerages, a copy of the foreign tax return with a payment confirmation, or a letter from the foreign tax authority confirming the amount withheld. The NTA will not simply take your word for it. If you receive dividends from a US brokerage, the IRS Form 1042-S or your brokerage's annual tax statement showing withholding is generally accepted. If you own UK rental property, a Self Assessment payment receipt from HMRC works. If documents are in a foreign language, some tax offices will request a simple translation, though practice varies. ---

**What if you live in a country with a tax treaty with Japan?** Japan has tax treaties with over 70 countries. Treaties often cap the withholding rate a foreign country can apply to certain income types. If the foreign country withheld more than the treaty rate, only the treaty-rate amount is creditable. The excess is technically recoverable from the foreign country, not Japan. You cannot credit the overtaxed portion against Japanese tax. **What if you paid tax in a country Japan does not have a treaty with?** The foreign tax credit still applies. Japan's domestic law provides the credit regardless of whether a treaty exists. The calculation and documentation requirements are the same. ---

The most common error: **not reporting the foreign income at all**. Some people assume that because tax was already withheld abroad, they do not need to declare it in Japan. That is wrong. Failure to report foreign income is a tax evasion risk, not a minor paperwork oversight. Japan has exchanged financial information with foreign tax authorities under the Common Reporting Standard since 2018. The second most common error: claiming the full amount of foreign tax paid rather than the creditable limit. The formula exists for a reason. Claiming more than the limit will result in a correction notice from the NTA. Third: treating the resident tax credit and income tax credit as the same thing and only filing one calculation. They are separate. The income tax credit offsets your national tax. The resident tax credit offsets the 10% flat municipal and prefectural tax. They need separate entries on the form. **Amending a return you already filed:** If you filed without claiming the credit, you have five years from the original filing date to submit a corrected return (更正の請求). This is not a penalty situation. You are requesting a reduction of tax already assessed. The process involves submitting a correction request form to the same tax office where you originally filed. Bring your original return, the correction form, and all supporting documents for the foreign tax credit you missed. The refund, if approved, is typically processed within several months. Then run the creditable limit formula before you sit down to file. Knowing your number in advance means the 外国税額控除に関する明細書 takes minutes to complete rather than becoming a reason to skip the credit entirely. ---

**Can I claim the foreign tax credit if I am still a non-permanent resident?** Yes, but only on foreign-source income that was remitted to Japan and taxed here. If the foreign income stayed abroad and was never brought into Japan, it is not subject to Japanese tax at the non-permanent resident stage, so there is no double taxation to offset. **What happens if I miss the March 15 deadline entirely?** You can still file and claim the credit late. A late filing penalty applies, but the credit itself is not forfeited. If you owe tax, delinquent interest accrues from the original due date. File as soon as possible after the deadline rather than waiting. **Does the foreign tax credit affect my resident tax bill the following year?** Yes. The resident tax credit section of the detail statement reduces your住民税 (resident tax) assessed by your municipality. If you omit that section, your June resident tax notice will not reflect the credit.
